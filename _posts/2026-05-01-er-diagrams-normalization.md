When Dr. Bilal Ahmad first drew an ER Diagram on the board, I 
stared at it with complete confusion. Boxes connected by lines, 
diamonds in the middle, attributes hanging off the sides like 
branches of a tree. It looked more like an art project than 
something related to databases. But as Dr. Bilal Ahmad began 
explaining each element one by one, something remarkable happened 
— it started making sense.

ER Diagrams — Entity Relationship Diagrams — are the blueprints 
of a database. Before you write a single line of SQL, before you 
create a single table, you design an ER Diagram. It shows you 
what entities exist in your system, what attributes they have, 
and most importantly — how they relate to each other. That last 
part, the relationships, was where I initially struggled the most.

Relationships in databases are not always obvious. One customer 
can place many orders. One order can contain many products. One 
student can enroll in many courses. Understanding these 
connections — one to one, one to many, many to many — required 
a shift in thinking. I had to stop thinking about individual 
pieces of data and start thinking about how everything connects 
to everything else. Once that shift happened, ER Diagrams stopped 
being confusing and started being genuinely interesting.

Dr. Bilal Ahmad had an exceptional way of explaining relationships. 
He always used real world scenarios that made the logic immediately 
clear. Instead of abstract examples, he would take situations from 
everyday life and show us exactly how they translated into database 
relationships. That approach made everything click much faster than 
I expected.

Normalization came next and surprisingly it felt more accessible 
than I had anticipated. The concept of organizing data to reduce 
redundancy and improve structure — breaking tables down into their 
most efficient form — followed a clear logical progression. First 
Normal Form, Second Normal Form, Third Normal Form — each level 
built naturally on the previous one. Dr. Bilal Ahmad walked us 
through all of them with patience and clarity, and by the end I 
felt genuinely comfortable with the entire process.

What made this topic special was how everything connected. ER 
Diagrams showed us how to design a database. Normalization showed 
us how to refine that design. Together they gave us a complete 
picture of what good database design actually looks like — 
structured, efficient, and free of unnecessary repetition.

Looking back, ER Diagrams and Normalization are two of the most 
valuable things I learned this semester. They taught me that 
before you build anything, you need to think carefully about 
structure and relationships. That lesson goes far beyond databases 
— it applies to everything in engineering and in life.
