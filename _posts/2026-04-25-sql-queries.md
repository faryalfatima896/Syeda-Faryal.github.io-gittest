Out of everything I studied this semester, SQL was one of those 
subjects that surprisingly did not feel as scary as I expected. 
When Dr. Bilal Ahmad first introduced us to SQL in our Database 
Systems course, something about it just clicked. Unlike Python which 
took me weeks to feel comfortable with, SQL felt logical and readable 
from the very beginning. You write what you want, and the database 
gives it to you. Simple. Clean. Powerful.

The basics came quickly. SELECT, INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE — the 
fundamental commands that every database developer lives by. Learning 
these felt like learning a new way to ask questions. Instead of 
searching through data manually, you write a query and let the 
database do the work. That concept alone felt like a superpower.

JOINs were where things got more interesting and more challenging. 
Combining data from multiple tables, understanding which type of JOIN 
to use, making sure the relationships between tables were correct — 
this required a different kind of thinking. But Dr. Bilal Ahmad had 
a way of making even complex concepts approachable. He always taught 
through scenario based examples — real situations where you actually 
needed to JOIN tables to answer a meaningful question. That approach 
made all the difference. Instead of memorizing syntax, I was 
understanding purpose.

The lab sessions were where everything became real. We connected SQL 
to a proper server environment and worked with actual database 
management tools. Installing and setting everything up was itself 
a learning experience — there were steps that confused me, settings 
that needed to be just right, and moments where nothing seemed to 
work. But getting through the installation process and seeing 
everything connect properly was deeply satisfying.

Working in the lab with SQL connected to a server felt completely 
different from just writing queries on paper. Suddenly the database 
was real. The data was real. The queries had actual results that 
appeared on screen in real time. That hands-on experience of writing 
a query, running it, and seeing the correct data appear — it made 
everything we had learned in theory come alive.

What SQL taught me goes beyond just database commands. It taught me 
how to think about data — how it is structured, how tables relate 
to each other, how to ask the right questions to get the right 
answers. Dr. Bilal Ahmad always emphasized understanding the logic 
behind what we were doing, not just copying commands. And that 
approach gave me a foundation in database thinking that I know will 
serve me for years to come.

SQL may have felt easier than some other subjects this semester — 
but that does not mean it was not valuable. Sometimes the lessons 
that come naturally are the ones that stick the deepest.
